# Teaching examples: from five answers to an English abstract

[简体中文](zh/examples.md) | English

Both studies, all numbers, and all abstracts below are fictional teaching materials created for this project.
They do not describe real papers or provide evidence of actual editing effectiveness.
Assume the research and Introduction are complete and the author has confirmed the listed content.
Learn from the content choices and organization; do not transfer the facts or sentences into a user's paper.

## Example 1: a main result, mechanism clues, and a longer-term result

### The author's five answers

| Part | Supplied research content |
|---|---|
| W | Can public transit subsidies improve employment among low-income job seekers? |
| H | In a large city, 2,400 low-income job seekers are randomly assigned to three months of transit subsidies or no subsidy. |
| F1 | Six months after assignment, employment is 6 percentage points higher in the subsidy group than in the control group. |
| F2 | Subsidy recipients attend 25 percent more job interviews. The paper interprets this as evidence consistent with eased travel constraints; it does not identify a causal mediation effect. |
| F3 | Twelve months after assignment, the employment gap remains 6 percentage points. Subsidies were provided for only three months. |

### English abstract

> This paper examines whether public transit subsidies improve employment among low-income job seekers. We randomly assign 2,400 job seekers in a large city to receive three months of transit subsidies or no subsidy. Subsidies increase employment by 6 percentage points six months after assignment. Recipients attend 25 percent more job interviews, consistent with an easing of travel constraints during job search. The employment gain remains at 6 percentage points after twelve months, well beyond the subsidy period.

### Why this arrangement works

- W states the question directly; H explains the random assignment, population, and intervention.
- F1 answers W through employment, including the magnitude and time horizon.
- F2 adds evidence about the job-search process. `Consistent with` preserves the author's assessment of the mechanism evidence.
- F3 reports persistence. It changes how readers understand a short subsidy and adds information absent from F1.

### Revising a flawed draft

Suppose an initial draft for the same study reads:

> Transportation is an important issue in modern society. This paper uses a rigorous method to explore an important policy. The policy increases employment by 6 percent. These findings demonstrate that travel constraints are the sole cause of unemployment. The policy therefore has important implications for policymakers.

Using the supplied research, the main changes are:

1. Replace generic background with the specific question, and `a rigorous method` with the actual randomized design.
2. Restore `6 percentage points` in place of `6 percent`, and restore the six-month time horizon.
3. Remove the unsupported claim `the sole cause of unemployment`. Use the interview result and the appropriately qualified mechanism interpretation.
4. Replace generic policy significance with the documented twelve-month employment result.

The revised text is the five-sentence abstract above.
The reasons for revising come from the research materials and the information missing for readers.

## Example 2: no mechanism evidence, with heterogeneity and another result

### The author's five answers

| Part | Supplied research content |
|---|---|
| W | How is firm-provided training related to labor productivity? |
| H | A survey of 800 manufacturing firms is linked to administrative records for the same year. The comparison is descriptive, with no causal identification design. |
| F1 | Firms offering training have 7 percent higher labor productivity on average. |
| F2 | No mechanism was tested. Another important finding is size heterogeneity: the productivity gap is 10 percent among large firms and 3 percent among small firms. |
| F3 | Firms offering training have annual employee turnover rates that are 2 percentage points lower. The paper does not test whether turnover explains the productivity gap. |

### English abstract

> This paper examines the relationship between firm-provided training and labor productivity. We link survey data from 800 manufacturing firms to administrative records for the same year. Firms offering training have 7 percent higher labor productivity on average. The productivity gap is 10 percent among large firms and 3 percent among small firms. Firms offering training also have annual employee turnover rates that are 2 percentage points lower.

### Why this arrangement works

- F1 answers the question about training and productivity using descriptive language that preserves the study's strength of evidence.
- F2 adds size differences to the average result. It does not invent untested explanations about skills or management quality to fill a mechanism sentence.
- F3 adds employee turnover as a distinct outcome. Do not recast it as the mechanism through which training raises productivity.

There is no separate significance sentence; the last substantive result completes the paragraph.
If space is tighter, compare the importance of size differences and turnover to the author's central contribution
before choosing which to retain. Do not remove one mechanically to fit a template.
