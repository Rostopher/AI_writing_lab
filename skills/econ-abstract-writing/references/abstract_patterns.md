# WHFFF: content roles and structure choices

[简体中文](zh/abstract_patterns.md) | English

Read this when the five answers are ready and you need to choose an order or shorten the abstract.

## Default arrangement

| Part | Purpose | Content to select |
|---|---|---|
| W | State the central question | Name the subject and relationship so readers quickly understand what the paper asks. |
| H | Explain the key method | For empirical work, emphasize the design that answers the question; for theory, state the key model, assumptions, or analytical method. |
| F1 | Give the direct answer | Preserve the main direction, magnitude, or proposition and the conditions needed to understand it. |
| F2 | Explain the main result further | Prioritize mechanism evidence; important heterogeneity, a contrast, or another finding can also serve this role. |
| F3 | Complete the findings | Add an important trade-off, longer-term result, economic consequence, or another finding that changes the reader's understanding. |

Choose the findings worth reporting before allocating space to background and methods.
Rank results by what they contribute to the research question, rather than by the order in which
regressions were run or tables appear.

## When to adjust the structure

| Situation | Possible arrangement | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| The question and design fit in one sentence, and space is tight | W+H → F1 → F2 → F3 | The combined sentence still makes the subject, question, and source of evidence clear. |
| The research design needs explanation | W+H → H → F1 → F2 → F3 | Each method sentence has a distinct job, such as stating the key comparison and explaining the data that make it possible. |
| Readers need specific context to understand the question | Brief context → W/H → F1 → F2 → F3 | The context supplies necessary information and quickly leads to this paper. |
| The paper has only one or two distinct findings | W → H → F1 → F2, or combine further | Do not split one result into repetitive statements or invent another to fill a sentence. |
| A theory paper embeds its method in the question or proposition's setup | W/model setup → main proposition → conditions or mechanism → implication | Do not force in data or an identification strategy; preserve conditions that matter to the result. |
| A specific implication is supported by the results | Connect the final finding naturally to that implication | State only the implication the study supports; an extra generic significance sentence is unnecessary. |

F2's “Why does this result arise?” is a mechanism question.
“Why does the research matter?” asks about significance.
Keeping these questions distinct helps avoid treating a generic contribution claim as a mechanism finding.

## Choosing the second and third findings

For each candidate, ask: Does it come from the paper? Does it add information?
What would the reader miss if it were removed?

- **Mechanism:** Which pathway was examined? Does the evidence establish the mechanism, or is it only consistent with it? Preserve that distinction.
- **Heterogeneity:** Where is the effect concentrated? Does the difference help answer the central question?
- **Contrasts and trade-offs:** Does the main result come with another important consequence in a different direction?
- **Longer-term results:** Does the effect persist, fade, or reverse? Preserve the observed time horizon.
- **Economic consequences:** Were the welfare, cost-benefit, efficiency, or distributional results actually estimated or derived?

Select the most important item or a few items. These categories are options, not slots that must all be filled.
A result serving several functions can be stated in one sentence.

## Shortening an abstract

First remove generic background, repeated conclusions, method details that can stay in the paper,
and vague significance statements. Then consider combining W/H or removing a secondary finding.
Preserve the main result's magnitude and unit, the key comparison, the study's scope, and any conditions
that change the meaning of the conclusion.
Use clearer verbs to reduce roundabout prose; do not compress several sentences into a chain of clauses.

The word target comes from the author or verified submission requirements.
Corpus word and sentence distributions only help estimate space.
